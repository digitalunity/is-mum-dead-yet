01 / THE DOORBELL

Someone's at the door.
But can Mum get there?

For my mum, the doorbell isn't simply about knowing someone has arrived. She moves very slowly, and a visitor or delivery driver might leave long before she can reach the door.

The idea is to bring several clues together: a doorbell press, an optional image from the doorbell, and the trolley's most recent estimated room. If the trolley appears to be in the bathroom or bedroom, that may help explain why the door isn't being answered.

That's different from a generic “Someone rang the bell” notification. It's context that could help us respond usefully.

The Tapo doorbell integration and automatic image attachment still need real-world testing. Images may be available separately in the doorbell app, and room estimates may be delayed or uncertain.

02 / HELPING FROM AFAR

What if I could answer the door for her?

It's not only about knowing that someone is there. If Mum can't reach the door quickly, a video doorbell's own app may let a family member look, listen and speak to the person outside.

For an ordinary delivery, we could ask the driver to leave it somewhere agreed. For something heavy or important, we could help coordinate a safe handover with Mum or a trusted visitor, following arrangements she's happy with.

The dream is to pair that human conversation with a little extra information about Mum's likely whereabouts, without putting another device in her hands.

💬

The difference it makes

Without context: “Someone rang. Nobody answered.”

With context: “Mum's trolley is estimated to be in the bedroom. I can check who's there and help with the delivery.”

Two-way conversation depends on the doorbell's supported app and connectivity. HomePulse doesn't promise to operate its audio or unlock a door.

03 / VISITORS & ENTRY

Did somebody arrive?
Did they come in?

Some visitors ring the doorbell; trusted people may have their own arrangements for entering without ringing at all. A delivery driver may approach and leave without opening the door.

That's why I want to combine outdoor camera events, the physical front-door sensor, and the trolley's last estimated room. A camera might show an arrival; a door contact might confirm the door was opened. If the trolley appears to be away from the entrance, that gives us an extra question to ask.

Where an outdoor recording genuinely shows a visitor using an agreed key-access arrangement, it may help explain what happened. But a camera that missed the action, a door contact event, or a lack of doorbell press cannot prove how somebody entered or who they were.

Over a day, those events could build a rough visitor timeline. That's useful context, not a measurement of meaningful social contact.

04 / WHERE IS MUM?

Why track the trolley, not the person?

My mum is housebound and relies on a walking trolley to move around the house independently. It carries things for her, including her food. That makes the trolley a particularly useful clue: she generally needs it whenever she moves between rooms.

A tiny Bluetooth beacon underneath it, with small receivers around the house, could give a rough room-level view of where the trolley is. No wearable, no phone to carry and no button she has to remember to press.

Because the trolley is such a strong part of Mum's mobility, its movements are a much better proxy than they would be in many other homes. It's still the trolley we measure. It might be left somewhere, someone else might move it, and Bluetooth readings can be wrong. So we should never treat an estimate as proof of Mum's exact location or health.

⌁

A clue that fits her routine

That's what I like about this approach. It adapts to the way Mum already lives instead of asking her to adapt to another gadget.

The beacon-and-receiver location model is a design being calibrated and tested, not a precise indoor positioning service.

05 / LITTLE SIGNS OF LIFE

Did anything happen overnight?

Movement isn't the only clue. We fitted a simple physical button to Mum's trolley so she could switch her lamp on without bending or reaching for an awkward switch.

That little accessibility improvement produces something unexpectedly valuable: a timestamp of a deliberate button press. We can say the button was used. We can't prove who pressed it or whether anyone is well now, but it is more meaningful than a random sensor blip.

A future timeline could combine that with confident trolley movement, door events and other interactions. The aim is to answer, “What's the latest positive sign of ordinary activity?”

✳

One little button. Two jobs.

Today: a real, working way for Mum to switch her lamp on and off.

For HomePulse: a useful resident-linked event we're planning to integrate into the broader activity picture.

Lamp-control hardware works today; HomePulse event ingestion and its dashboard are not yet proven end-to-end.

06 / UNUSUALLY QUIET

Is today quieter than normal?

We're interested in patterns, not arbitrary “no movement for 30 minutes” alarms. An ordinary afternoon nap is very different from a change in someone's usual routine.

Over time, the idea is to learn when the trolley usually moves, when controls are normally used, and whether anything feels notably different. An alert would say what was observed, how recent the data is, and which devices were actually working.

Sometimes the answer will simply be “we don't know, the receiver is offline”. That matters just as much as the activity itself.

♡

What an alert should mean

“There's been less activity than we'd usually expect. Could be a nap, a change of routine or a sensor issue. Maybe give Mum a call.”

Pattern-based alerts are a future design goal, not a safety service. Lack of movement is not evidence of death.

07 / ANOTHER SMALL CLUE

Does her phone tell us anything?

With family location sharing already agreed, I can sometimes see the phone's battery level in Google Maps. Whether it's charged, charging or slowly draining adds a little context without making Mum answer another question.

But phone battery data is a weak signal. A phone can charge untouched for hours, run flat on a table, or stop reporting altogether. Battery level isn't evidence that someone has moved or is safe.

It's an example of thinking about information we already have. This is a separate, optional family observation, not a HomePulse Google Maps integration.

▰

Useful, but limited

“The phone has battery” is handy to know before calling. It doesn't tell us whether Mum has seen it, charged it herself or is carrying it.

Any sharing should be consensual and follow the relevant app's privacy settings.

08 / WHY THIS APPROACH?

Because dignity is part of the design.

Of course, it would be possible to put cameras in every room and microphones around the house. You could see and hear far more. But that isn't the point, and it isn't the kind of experience I want for Mum.

We want enough context to know when to check in, while leaving her home feeling like her home. The beacon is on something she already uses. The button solves an everyday problem. Door and outdoor camera events can help with visitors without filming her private indoor life.

Collect only what helps, protect it carefully, retain as little as practical, and be honest about what the information cannot tell us. That balance matters more than building the cleverest monitoring system.

Her home.
Her dignity.
Our care.

THE ACTUAL POINT

It's not one magic gadget.
It's the combination.

Every signal has a useful job, and every signal has something it cannot prove.

⌁ Trolley beacon

May help estimate the trolley's room, not Mum's exact position.

✳ Lamp button

Records a real control interaction, not who pressed it or their wellbeing.

⌂ Door contact

Records opening and closing, not visitor identity.

▧ Outdoor camera

May give visual context, but can miss arrivals and cannot see everything.

▰ Phone battery

Shows an optional device clue, not human activity.

♡ Human check-in

Still the most important part: call, visit, or arrange appropriate help.
